# 10 Practical Ways to Increase Average Order Value Without Simply Raising Prices

## Introduction

Increasing average order value is one of the most useful levers in ecommerce growth, but it is also one of the easiest metrics to optimise in the wrong direction.

A store can increase AOV while becoming less profitable.

For example, imagine an ecommerce store with an AOV of AED 200 and a conversion rate of 3%. It introduces aggressive upsells and raises AOV to AED 240, but conversion rate falls to 2.3%.

The higher AOV looks impressive in isolation. The economics do not.

This is why AOV should not be treated as simply a merchandising metric. For performance marketers, it is part of the economics of the entire acquisition funnel.

The better question is not:

> How can we make customers spend more?

It is:

> How can we make the existing purchase decision more valuable and more logical for the customer?

That distinction changes how you approach bundles, upsells, cross sells, shipping thresholds, product recommendations and even landing pages.

## What Average Order Value Actually Measures

Average Order Value, or AOV, measures the average revenue generated per completed order.

The basic formula is:

**AOV = Total Revenue ÷ Number of Orders**

For example, if an ecommerce store generates AED 50,000 from 250 orders:

**AOV = AED 50,000 ÷ 250 = AED 200**

Google also defines average order value in essentially the same way within its advertising measurement framework: total revenue divided by total orders.

AOV tells you how much customers are spending per transaction.

It does not tell you whether those customers are profitable.

That distinction matters because revenue can increase while contribution margin decreases, acquisition costs rise or conversion rates fall.

## Why AOV Is Useful but Dangerous to Read in Isolation

Suppose two stores have the following performance:

| Metric | Store A | Store B |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Visitors | 10,000 | 10,000 |
| Conversion rate | 3% | 2.3% |
| Orders | 300 | 230 |
| AOV | AED 200 | AED 240 |
| Revenue | AED 60,000 | AED 55,200 |
| Revenue per visitor | AED 6.00 | AED 5.52 |

Store B increased AOV by AED 40.

It also generated less revenue from the same amount of traffic.

This is why AOV optimisation should be connected to **revenue per visitor**.

Revenue per visitor can be expressed simply as:

**Revenue per visitor = Revenue ÷ Visitors**

In this example, the AOV improvement was not enough to compensate for the decline in conversion.

The same principle applies to profitability.

If an upsell adds AED 30 of revenue but costs AED 22 in product, fulfilment, payment and other variable costs, the business has not gained AED 30 of economic value.

It has gained something closer to the incremental contribution generated after those costs.

The metrics worth watching therefore include:

1. AOV
2. Conversion rate
3. Revenue per visitor
4. Gross profit per order
5. Contribution margin
6. Customer acquisition cost
7. Repeat purchase rate
8. Customer lifetime value

Google Analytics supports ecommerce purchase and revenue measurement, including purchase revenue, transactions and average purchase revenue metrics, which makes accurate ecommerce instrumentation an important foundation for this type of analysis.

The objective is not maximum AOV.

The objective is **better economics per unit of traffic and customer**.

# 1. Build Bundles That Solve a Real Customer Problem

Product bundles are one of the clearest ways to increase AOV because they can increase the value of the transaction without requiring customers to search for complementary products themselves.

But a bundle should not simply be:

**Product A + Product B + Product C**

A stronger bundle answers a customer need.

Consider a hypothetical skincare store.

A customer purchasing a cleanser may also need a moisturiser and sunscreen.

Instead of presenting three unrelated products, the store could offer:

**Daily Skincare Routine**

Cleanser  
Moisturiser  
SPF

The bundle creates a logical solution rather than an arbitrary collection of products.

The same principle can apply to other categories.

A coffee machine could be bundled with coffee beans and a descaling solution.

A camera could be bundled with a memory card and spare battery.

A baby product store could create a newborn essentials package.

A fitness retailer could create a home workout starter kit.

The strategic question is:

**What products naturally belong together in the customer's decision?**

That is more useful than asking which products have the highest individual margins.

### Measure the bundle beyond AOV

Track:

1. Bundle take rate
2. Conversion rate
3. Revenue per visitor
4. Gross profit per order
5. Contribution margin
6. Return rate where relevant

A bundle that increases AOV but reduces profitability is not necessarily a successful bundle.

# 2. Use Upsells Only When the Upgrade Is Obvious

Upselling and cross selling are often treated as the same tactic. They are not.

An **upsell** encourages the customer to purchase a higher value version of the product they already want.

A **cross sell** introduces another product that complements the original purchase.

For example:

**Upsell**

Customer selects a 500 ml coffee machine.

Offer a 1.5 litre premium model with additional functionality.

**Cross sell**

Customer buys the coffee machine.

Recommend compatible coffee beans or a cleaning kit.

The distinction matters because the customer's mental decision is different.

An effective upsell should make the additional spend easy to understand.

For example:

**Standard Camera**

AED 2,400

**Creator Kit**

AED 2,850

Includes additional battery, memory card and compact microphone.

The question is not whether AED 450 can be extracted from the customer.

The question is whether the additional value is sufficiently clear to justify AED 450.

This is where timing matters.

A relevant upsell can appear:

1. On the product page
2. After product selection
3. In the cart
4. During checkout where technically appropriate
5. Immediately after purchase

The closer the offer is to the original decision, the more important relevance and clarity become.

# 3. Make Cross Sells Contextual, Not Random

Poor cross selling looks like a catalogue.

A customer buys a camera and sees:

**You may also like:**

Five unrelated cameras.

That does not necessarily increase the value of the purchase.

Contextual cross selling instead asks:

**What is the customer's next logical need?**

For a camera:

Memory card  
Spare battery  
Camera bag  
Tripod

For a gym product:

Resistance bands  
Training gloves  
Workout mat

For a baby stroller:

Rain cover  
Travel bag  
Bottle holder

For a coffee machine:

Coffee beans  
Milk frother  
Cleaning tablets

The important distinction is between **product similarity** and **purchase relevance**.

The most visually similar product is not always the most commercially useful recommendation.

A good cross sell reduces the amount of additional research the customer has to do.

# 4. Set a Free Shipping Threshold Your Data Can Support

Free shipping thresholds can influence basket composition because they give customers a reason to consider adding another product.

Imagine a hypothetical store where many orders are around AED 170.

The store could test:

**Free shipping on orders above AED 200**

A customer with AED 170 in the cart now has a clear economic decision:

Pay the shipping cost, or add approximately AED 30 of additional merchandise.

But the threshold should not be selected simply because it is 20% or 30% above AOV.

Shipping economics, product margins, customer behaviour, order distribution and fulfilment costs all matter.

Shopify similarly recommends considering AOV, median order value, shipping costs and customer preferences when determining a free shipping minimum.

A threshold that is too low may provide free shipping without materially changing basket size.

A threshold that is too high may feel unattainable and create friction instead.

The right threshold is therefore a **testable commercial hypothesis**, not a universal formula.

### Make the threshold visible

Do not hide the condition in a site wide promotional banner.

Baymard's ecommerce UX research has found that users often look for shipping information during product evaluation and recommends making free shipping conditions clear near the purchase area.

A stronger message might be:

**AED 30 more for free shipping**

That is considerably more actionable than:

**Free shipping available**

The first communicates the customer's current position.

# 5. Show Progress Toward the Threshold

Once a free shipping threshold exists, show customers how close they are to reaching it.

For example:

**Cart total: AED 170**

**AED 30 more to unlock free shipping**

This turns an abstract promotion into a visible goal.

The same principle can work with other incentives:

**AED 50 more to unlock a free accessory**

**Add one more item to complete your routine**

**Spend AED 300 to unlock your member benefit**

However, the progress indicator should not become an obstacle to checkout.

The customer should always understand:

1. Current cart value
2. Remaining amount
3. Benefit received
4. Whether adding another product is optional

Baymard's testing specifically identifies dynamic free shipping messages, such as showing the remaining amount needed, as a useful experiment for ecommerce stores using shipping thresholds.

The important word is **experiment**.

A progress bar is not automatically beneficial simply because it looks persuasive.

# 6. Recommend Products Based on the Customer's Next Need

Product recommendations become more useful when they reflect context.

A customer browsing a coffee machine may have a different intent from a customer browsing coffee beans.

Similarly, someone purchasing running shoes may have a different next need from someone purchasing resistance bands.

Recommendation logic can use signals such as:

1. Product currently viewed
2. Products already added to cart
3. Category
4. Product compatibility
5. Quantity
6. Previous purchases
7. Customer segment
8. Current stage of the funnel

The goal is not to show more products.

It is to reduce decision effort.

Consider a customer buying a camera.

Instead of:

**Recommended for you**

Show:

**Complete your camera setup**

Memory card  
Spare battery  
Protective case

The second recommendation has a stronger relationship to the current purchase.

Personalisation can be valuable, but the underlying principle is more fundamental:

**Relevance beats volume.**

# 7. Offer Larger Packs or Multi Buy Options

Some products naturally lend themselves to quantity based purchasing.

Consumables are the obvious example.

Coffee  
Protein products  
Skincare  
Baby supplies  
Household products  
Pet products

Instead of selling one unit at a time, consider presenting:

**1 pack**

AED 60

**2 pack**

AED 110

**3 pack**

AED 150

The customer receives a better unit economics proposition while the business increases the value of the transaction.

The same concept can work outside consumables.

For apparel, it could be:

**Buy two basic T shirts**

For accessories:

**Any three accessories**

For fitness products:

**Home workout starter set**

The critical question is whether the customer has a genuine reason to purchase more quantity.

A multi buy offer can increase AOV while creating unnecessary inventory for customers if the product is rarely used or purchased.

That can increase dissatisfaction and returns.

# 8. Use Price Anchoring to Clarify Value, Not Manipulate

Price anchoring is about giving customers a reference point that helps them evaluate an offer.

Consider a coffee machine store presenting:

**Essential**

AED 599

**Popular**

AED 799

**Premium**

AED 1,099

The middle option may become easier to evaluate because the customer can compare it against both a lower and higher reference point.

But anchoring should be honest.

Do not create artificial reference prices.

Do not claim a product was previously sold at a higher price when it was not.

Do not use inflated comparisons simply to make the target product appear inexpensive.

The purpose of anchoring should be to answer:

**What do I get for the additional money?**

That means the product comparison should focus on meaningful differences:

More capacity  
Better materials  
Additional functionality  
More accessories  
Longer warranty  
Greater quantity

This is especially useful when building product pages and landing pages for paid traffic.

# 9. Build AOV Into the Landing Page and Ad Funnel

AOV optimisation does not begin after the customer reaches the cart.

It can begin with the ad.

Suppose a Meta Ads campaign promotes a skincare product.

The advertisement focuses entirely on a single AED 100 product.

The landing page then introduces a three product routine for AED 250.

There is a potential mismatch between the customer's initial expectation and the commercial offer.

A better funnel can connect the acquisition message to the broader purchase solution.

For example:

**Meta Ad**

Build a simple daily skincare routine.

**Landing Page**

Step 1: Cleanse  
Step 2: Hydrate  
Step 3: Protect

**Offer**

Complete routine: AED 250

**Product Page**

Individual products also available.

The same principle applies to Google Ads.

If someone searches for a specific product, the landing page should satisfy that intent first. The AOV strategy can then introduce relevant complementary products or bundles.

This is where performance marketing and ecommerce merchandising intersect.

Paid acquisition brings the visitor.

The landing page shapes the decision.

The product page creates confidence.

The cart expands the basket.

The checkout completes the transaction.

The post purchase experience can create additional value.

AOV is therefore not a single page optimisation problem.

It is a **funnel optimisation problem**.

For a performance marketer, this is particularly important because improving AOV can change the economic value of traffic already being purchased.

The right question becomes:

**Can we generate more contribution from the same qualified traffic without damaging conversion or customer experience?**

That is a much stronger growth question than simply trying to push the AOV number upward.

# 10. Use Post Purchase Offers Without Disrupting Checkout

One of the most interesting places to increase order value is after the customer has already completed the primary purchase.

The customer has made the main decision.

An appropriately designed post purchase offer can therefore introduce another relevant product or upgrade without changing the original checkout decision.

For example:

A customer purchases a coffee machine.

The confirmation experience could offer:

**Add a coffee bean starter pack to your order**

A customer buys a camera.

**Add a spare battery at a special bundle price**

A customer purchases a skincare routine.

**Add your next month's cleanser supply**

The offer should remain relevant and easy to understand.

It should also avoid creating confusion around the original transaction.

Post purchase offers are not a reason to bombard customers with additional promotions.

The strategic objective is simple:

**Identify the next logical purchase while the customer's buying context is still fresh.**

# How to Measure AOV Without Fooling Yourself

The biggest mistake in AOV optimisation is evaluating the tactic using AOV alone.

Instead, establish a measurement hierarchy.

### Level 1: Transaction value

Did AOV increase?

### Level 2: Conversion efficiency

Did conversion rate change?

### Level 3: Traffic economics

Did revenue per visitor increase?

### Level 4: Profitability

Did gross profit and contribution margin improve?

### Level 5: Customer economics

Did repeat purchasing and customer lifetime value improve?

This hierarchy prevents a common optimisation error.

Imagine a hypothetical ecommerce experiment:

**Before**

AOV: AED 200  
Conversion rate: 3%  
Visitors: 10,000

Orders:

10,000 × 3% = 300

Revenue:

300 × AED 200 = AED 60,000

Revenue per visitor:

AED 60,000 ÷ 10,000 = AED 6

Now introduce an aggressive upsell.

**After**

AOV: AED 240  
Conversion rate: 2.3%  
Visitors: 10,000

Orders:

10,000 × 2.3% = 230

Revenue:

230 × AED 240 = AED 55,200

Revenue per visitor:

AED 55,200 ÷ 10,000 = AED 5.52

AOV increased by 20%.

Revenue per visitor fell by 8%.

The AOV result is therefore misleading if considered alone.

This is an illustrative calculation, not a benchmark or expected outcome.

## AOV Also Needs a Margin Lens

Suppose an upsell adds AED 40 to an order.

That does not necessarily mean the business gained AED 40 in profit.

Consider:

Selling price: AED 40  
Product cost: AED 20  
Fulfilment cost: AED 5  
Payment and variable costs: AED 3

Illustrative incremental contribution:

**AED 40 − AED 20 − AED 5 − AED 3 = AED 12**

The exact calculation depends on the business's accounting model and cost structure.

The principle is what matters:

**Revenue is not contribution.**

This becomes especially important when discounts, bundles, free shipping and promotional incentives are involved.

# A Simple AOV Testing Framework

Instead of launching ten changes simultaneously, use a structured testing process.

## Step 1: Identify the economic problem

Start with the data.

Is AOV low because customers buy only one product?

Are complementary products being ignored?

Is shipping discouraging larger baskets?

Are customers unable to understand the difference between product tiers?

Are landing pages promoting individual products when a broader solution makes more sense?

## Step 2: Find the highest leverage opportunity

Look for a large number of transactions where a relatively small behavioural change could increase order value.

For example:

If 70% of orders contain only one product, bundles and contextual cross sells may deserve investigation.

If many carts sit just below the free shipping threshold, a threshold experiment may be relevant.

These are hypotheses, not universal rules.

## Step 3: Create one clear hypothesis

For example:

**If we show compatible accessories in the cart, customers will add more complementary products without materially reducing checkout completion.**

That is much better than:

**Let's add more upsells.**

## Step 4: Define primary and guardrail metrics

Primary metric:

**Revenue per visitor**

Supporting metric:

**AOV**

Guardrail metrics:

Conversion rate  
Contribution margin  
Refund rate  
Return rate  
Customer complaints

The exact metrics should reflect the business model.

## Step 5: Test placement and offer design

Do not only test whether an offer exists.

Test:

Offer positioning  
Product selection  
Bundle structure  
Copy  
Threshold  
Timing  
Quantity  
Price presentation

For example, a cross sell may perform differently on the product page compared with the cart.

## Step 6: Evaluate the whole funnel

After the experiment, ask:

Did AOV increase?

Did conversion rate change?

Did revenue per visitor improve?

Did contribution margin improve?

Did acquisition economics improve?

Did customers return or purchase again?

The winning variation is not necessarily the one with the highest AOV.

It is the one that improves the business's economics without creating unacceptable downstream costs.

# Common Mistakes That Damage Conversion or Margin

## Treating AOV as the main KPI

AOV is a diagnostic and optimisation metric.

It is not the entire business model.

## Adding too many recommendations

More recommendations can create more choice without creating more value.

## Making upsells irrelevant

A customer buying one product does not automatically want five more products.

Relevance matters.

## Setting an unrealistic free shipping threshold

If the threshold requires customers to spend substantially more than they normally would, the incentive may stop being attractive.

## Hiding shipping conditions

Customers should understand the conditions attached to a free shipping offer.

Baymard's research highlights the importance of making shipping information and threshold conditions visible during product evaluation.

## Discounting everything

A higher AOV achieved through heavy discounting can produce worse contribution margins.

## Optimising only for immediate revenue

Some products have strong repeat purchase economics.

A tactic that slightly reduces first order value but increases repeat purchasing could potentially be more valuable over the customer's lifetime.

That is why customer lifetime value should be considered where the business model supports meaningful repeat purchasing.

## Making the checkout harder

AOV tactics should never be allowed to undermine the primary purpose of the checkout: completing the purchase.

Baymard's ongoing checkout research demonstrates how checkout usability can materially affect ecommerce completion, which is why AOV experimentation should always include conversion and checkout behaviour as guardrails.

# The Five Part AOV Optimisation Framework

A practical way to think about AOV optimisation is:

**1. Understand**

Analyse order composition, product relationships, customer behaviour and traffic economics.

**2. Design**

Create a more valuable purchase structure through bundles, upgrades, recommendations or quantity options.

**3. Place**

Put the offer where it makes contextual sense across the landing page, product page, cart and post purchase journey.

**4. Test**

Measure AOV alongside conversion rate, revenue per visitor and contribution economics.

**5. Scale**

Only expand the tactic when the economics remain attractive across meaningful segments and traffic sources.

This framework turns AOV from a merchandising exercise into a performance marketing discipline.

# Final Thoughts

Increasing average order value is not fundamentally about convincing customers to spend more.

It is about making the purchase they are already considering more valuable.

A well designed bundle can solve a problem.

A relevant upsell can make a better product choice easier.

A contextual cross sell can anticipate the customer's next need.

A free shipping threshold can give customers a rational reason to add another item.

A better landing page can introduce the complete solution before the customer reaches the cart.

A post purchase offer can create additional value without interfering with the original decision.

But every one of these tactics has the same requirement:

**The economics must work beyond AOV.**

If AOV increases while conversion rate falls, revenue per visitor can decline.

If revenue increases while contribution margin falls, profitability can decline.

If first order value increases while repeat purchase behaviour deteriorates, customer economics can worsen.

That is why the strongest ecommerce operators do not optimise AOV in isolation.

They optimise the relationship between **traffic, conversion, order value, margin and customer lifetime value**.

For performance marketers, this is particularly important.

You cannot always acquire dramatically cheaper traffic.

You can, however, sometimes create more economic value from the traffic you already acquire.

That makes AOV more than an ecommerce reporting metric.

It becomes a lever in the wider performance marketing system.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is Average Order Value?

Average Order Value is the average revenue generated per completed order. The basic calculation is total revenue divided by the number of orders.

### How do you calculate AOV?

Use:

**AOV = Total Revenue ÷ Number of Orders**

For example, AED 20,000 in revenue across 100 orders produces an AOV of AED 200.

### Is increasing AOV always good?

No.

A higher AOV can be accompanied by lower conversion, lower revenue per visitor or lower contribution margin. AOV should therefore be evaluated alongside the broader economics of the funnel.

### What is the difference between upselling and cross selling?

Upselling encourages the customer to choose a higher value version of the product they are considering.

Cross selling recommends an additional product that complements the original purchase.

### Should ecommerce businesses focus on AOV or conversion rate?

Neither should automatically dominate the other.

The better question is which combination produces stronger revenue and contribution economics from the available traffic.

### How can I increase AOV without discounting?

You can test bundles, relevant upsells, contextual cross sells, larger packs, product recommendations, free shipping thresholds, product tiering and post purchase offers.

### Should I offer free shipping to increase AOV?

Possibly, but the threshold should be based on your economics and customer behaviour rather than a universal percentage above AOV.

### How should I measure an AOV experiment?

Track AOV alongside conversion rate, revenue per visitor, gross profit, contribution margin and relevant customer metrics such as repeat purchase rate and customer lifetime value.
